# MODULE 6 - STORY AND VISUALIZATION SELLING

Status: Working Draft - Internal Sales Training

## Purpose

Business owners do not always react strongly to abstract statements such as:

- improve lead response
- automate follow-up
- increase employee capacity
- integrate the CRM
- improve attribution

Those ideas become more understandable when the salesperson helps the owner see the workflow happening in the real world.

Story and visualization selling translates an operational problem into a simple sequence the prospect can picture.

The objective is not to manipulate the prospect.

The objective is to make a real business process easier to understand.

A good YAD story should help the prospect see:

CURRENT STATE
->
BREAKPOINT
->
BUSINESS CONSEQUENCE
->
BETTER WORKFLOW
->
MEASURABLE RESULT CATEGORY

The story is not proof.

The story creates understanding.

The prospect's own data creates the business case.

---

# 1. THE CORE RULE

Do not tell a dramatic story and then pretend it happened to a real client.

Every story used by YAD must fall into one of two categories:

## REAL CASE STUDY - VERIFIED

Use only when the company has verified the client, facts, implementation, and result and has approved the way the case study may be presented.

## HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

A clearly identified example used to illustrate how a workflow may create business value.

Recommended language:

> Let me give you a hypothetical example so you can see what I mean.

or:

> This is not one of your numbers yet - I just want to show you the workflow.

Never say:

> We had a client exactly like you that made $200,000 from this

unless the case study is verified and approved.

---

# 2. WHY STORIES WORK IN YAD SALES

A prospect may not immediately understand:

> lead-response automation

They can understand:

> A homeowner's AC stops cooling at 8:47 PM. They search Google, click an ad, call Company A, and get voicemail. They call Company B two minutes later, somebody answers, and Company B books the appointment.

Now the owner understands the business issue:

The advertising may have worked.

The lead existed.

The failure occurred after acquisition.

That creates a much better conversation.

---

# 3. THE FIVE-PART YAD STORY

Use:

## 1. PERSON

Who is experiencing the workflow?

Examples:

- homeowner
- prospective legal client
- collision customer
- office employee
- salesperson
- manager
- patient / treatment prospect

## 2. TRIGGER

What happened?

Examples:

- AC stopped cooling
- accident occurred
- homeowner requested a roofing estimate
- lead submitted a form
- employee needs information
- manager needs a report

## 3. BREAKPOINT

Where does the current process fail or become inconsistent?

Examples:

- voicemail
- delayed response
- no second follow-up
- manual data transfer
- nobody owns the lead
- report takes hours to assemble

## 4. CONSEQUENCE

What business exposure may result?

Examples:

- opportunity moves to competitor
- estimate dies
- employee loses productive time
- customer receives poor communication
- management cannot see performance

## 5. BETTER WORKFLOW

What could happen instead?

Examples:

- immediate acknowledgment
- routing
- scheduling
- CRM task
- automated follow-up
- human escalation
- dashboard update

Do not end the story with:

> And then AI makes you rich.

End with a question.

> How close is that to what happens in your company today?

---

# 4. STORY -> QUESTION -> DATA

A YAD story should not become a five-minute monologue.

Use this sequence:

STORY
->
RELEVANCE QUESTION
->
PROCESS QUESTION
->
FREQUENCY
->
VALUE

Example:

> Let me give you a hypothetical. It's 8:47 PM and a homeowner's AC stops cooling. They click your Google ad and call. If nobody answers, they call the next company. How close is that to what happens here after hours?

Owner:

> Pretty close. It goes to voicemail.

Rep:

> Roughly how many after-hours calls do you get in a normal month?

Now the story has become diagnosis.

---

# 5. STORY LENGTH

Use three levels.

## 15-SECOND STORY

Best for cold calls.

> Homeowner clicks a paid ad, calls after hours, gets voicemail, calls the next company. What happens to that call at your company today?

## 45-SECOND STORY

Best once the owner is engaged.

Add the customer journey and breakpoint.

## 2-MINUTE STORY

Use during discovery, presentation, or in-person conversation when the prospect has given permission for a deeper explanation.

Do not use a two-minute story on a receptionist who is trying to answer the phone.

# 8. THE CUSTOMER JOURNEY RACE

Use this for speed-driven industries.

Hypothetical visual:

CUSTOMER NEED
|
+-- Company A: Ad -> call -> voicemail -> callback tomorrow
|
+-- Company B: Ad -> answer -> qualify -> schedule tonight

Question:

> If both companies are equally good technically, which one gets more opportunities simply because of the process?

Do not claim every customer always chooses the fastest responder.

The point is that response speed can affect opportunity capture.

# 9. HVAC STORY - THE 8:47 PM CALL

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> It's 8:47 PM in July. A homeowner walks into a 79-degree house and the AC isn't cooling. They search Google, see your ad, and call. The office is closed, so the call reaches voicemail. They don't want to wait until morning. They hit Back and call the next company. That company answers, gets the address, and books the service call. Your Google ad did its job. The failure happened after the click.

Then ask:

> What actually happens to that 8:47 PM call here today?

Possible visual:

$ PAID SEARCH
↓
PHONE CALL
↓
VOICEMAIL
↓
CUSTOMER CALLS COMPETITOR

Do not put a dollar amount on the lost job until the prospect provides defensible numbers.

---

# 10. PLUMBING STORY - THREE CALLS AT ONCE

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> It's Monday morning. The dispatcher is speaking to a customer. A second line rings and another employee grabs it. A third new customer calls with a leak and gets voicemail. That caller doesn't know the office is busy - they just know they need a plumber.

Ask:

> When that third call happens here, what system makes sure it gets recovered?

Improved-state visual:

CALL OVERFLOW
->
CAPTURE
->
TEXT / CALLBACK WORKFLOW
->
CRM
->
HUMAN FOLLOW-UP

The human team remains central.

---

# 11. ROOFING STORY - THE ESTIMATE GRAVEYARD

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A roofing salesperson inspects a house and sends a $20,000 proposal. The homeowner says they want to think about it. The salesperson has ten more inspections that week. The estimate sits in the CRM. No defined follow-up sequence exists. Thirty days later, nobody can clearly explain whether the homeowner chose a competitor, delayed the project, or simply forgot.

Ask:

> How close is that to what happens with your unsold estimates?

Visual:

INSPECTION
->
PROPOSAL
->
"THINK ABOUT IT"
->
NO OWNER
->
NO FOLLOW-UP
->
UNKNOWN

Better workflow:

PROPOSAL
->
SCHEDULED FOLLOW-UP
->
SMS / EMAIL SUPPORT
->
SALESPERSON TASK
->
STATUS
->
WIN / LOSS REASON

---

# 12. COLLISION REPAIR STORY - THE FRONT DESK BOTTLENECK

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A customer has just been in an accident and calls for an estimate. At the same time the CSR is helping a customer pick up a vehicle, answering an insurance question, and receiving a parts call. The new estimate call gets missed. The customer then calls another body shop.

Ask:

> Does the shop have visibility into how often that happens?

Then:

> What happens automatically when a new estimate call is missed?

This story attacks the workflow.

Not the CSR.

Approved framing:

> The front desk may be doing exactly what it should. The question is whether the system around the front desk has enough capacity for the volume.

---

# 13. LAW FIRM STORY - THE AFTER-HOURS INTAKE

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> Someone has a serious legal problem at 9:30 PM. They search for an attorney and contact several firms. One firm responds the next morning. Another immediately acknowledges the inquiry, collects basic non-legal intake information, schedules a consultation, and routes the matter for human review.

Ask:

> What happens to a new inquiry after your intake team goes home?

Important:

AI must not be represented as providing unauthorized legal advice.

Visual:

INQUIRY
->
ACKNOWLEDGMENT
->
BASIC INTAKE
->
HUMAN REVIEW
->
CONSULTATION

---

# 14. DENTAL / MED SPA STORY - THE UNBOOKED INQUIRY

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A prospect asks about implants, cosmetic treatment, or a med-spa procedure. The front desk answers the first question, but the prospect says they need to check their schedule. Nobody owns the follow-up. Two weeks later, the inquiry is buried under newer leads.

Ask:

> What happens to somebody who expresses interest but does not book during the first conversation?

Visual:

INQUIRY
->
CONVERSATION
->
NOT READY
->
FOLLOW-UP?

The question mark is the leak.

Clinical decisions stay human.

---

# 15. ASSISTED LIVING STORY - THE FAMILY THAT IS NOT READY YET

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A daughter calls because her father may need assisted living, but the family is still waiting on rehab and does not know the discharge date. She is not ready to tour today. If that inquiry simply gets marked "not ready," the facility may lose the relationship by the time the family actually needs a room.

Ask:

> How do you stay in touch with families whose need is real but the timing is not immediate?

Visual:

FAMILY INQUIRY
->
NOT READY TODAY
->
NURTURE / CHECK-IN
->
TOUR
->
DECISION

Sensitive care and family decisions remain human.

---

# 16. THE EMPLOYEE CAPACITY STORY

This is one of the most important YAD stories.

Do not make the employee the villain.

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> Imagine your best office coordinator spends the first hour of every morning copying yesterday's leads into a spreadsheet, updating the CRM, sending the same reminder messages, and assembling a report for management. The employee may be excellent. The question is whether those tasks require an excellent employee's judgment every single time.

Ask:

> If we gave that employee five hours back every week, where would you want those hours spent instead?

Visual:

BEFORE

Good employee
->
Data entry
->
Copy/paste
->
Reminders
->
Report assembly

AFTER

Systems handle routine work
->
Employee handles customers
->
Exceptions
->
Sales support
->
Problem solving
->
Higher-value work

The result is capacity.

Not automatic payroll savings.

---

# 17. THE GROWTH WITHOUT ONE-FOR-ONE OVERHEAD STORY

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A company grows from 500 leads a month to 750. Under the current process, management assumes it needs another coordinator because every new lead creates manual entry, follow-up, scheduling, reminders, and reporting work. Before hiring, YAD maps the workflow and asks which portion can be handled more efficiently by better systems.

The positioning is:

> Can the current team handle more volume with better systems?

Not:

> How do we eliminate the next employee?

---

# 18. THE CRM STORY - FROM STICKY NOTES TO A SYSTEM

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A lead calls. One employee writes the name down. Another salesperson texts the customer from a personal phone. Nobody records the result. Three days later the owner asks what happened to the lead and nobody knows.

Then show:

CALL / FORM / AD
->
CRM RECORD
->
OWNER / ASSIGNMENT
->
TASK
->
COMMUNICATION HISTORY
->
NEXT STEP
->
OUTCOME
->
REPORTING

Ask:

> If I picked any open lead in your company right now, could we see that entire history in one place?

This is how a salesperson explains CRM value without lecturing about software.

# 19. THE MARKETING BLACK BOX STORY

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> The owner spends money on Google and Meta. The dashboard shows leads. The CRM shows customers. Accounting shows revenue. But nobody can reliably connect the three. Marketing says the campaign is working because leads are cheap. Sales says the leads are bad. Ownership does not know which side is correct.

Visual:

AD PLATFORM
X
CRM
X
REVENUE

Then show:

AD
->
LEAD SOURCE
->
CRM
->
QUALIFIED
->
SALE
->
REVENUE

Ask:

> Can you currently trace a marketing dollar all the way to a sold customer?

# 20. THE $500 LEAD THAT DIED IN VOICEMAIL

This is a concept, not a benchmark.

Do not say a lead actually costs $500 unless that is supported by the prospect's data.

Use:

> Let's say, purely for illustration, your customer-acquisition process represents $500 of investment per qualified opportunity. If that opportunity reaches voicemail and nobody recovers it, the business did not only lose a possible customer. It also paid to create the opportunity that disappeared.

Then immediately return to the prospect's numbers:

> What does a qualified opportunity actually cost you?

This illustrates the double-loss concept without inventing spend.

---

# 21. THE NO-SHOW STORY

HYPOTHETICAL BUSINESS SCENARIO

> A practice has a one-hour appointment blocked. The patient never arrives. The employee time and schedule capacity were reserved, another patient may have wanted the slot, and the practice still has to decide whether to chase the person manually.

Ask:

> What happens before and after a no-show today?

Possible improved workflow:

BOOKING
->
CONFIRMATION
->
REMINDER
->
RESCHEDULE OPTION
->
NO-SHOW FOLLOW-UP
->
REBOOK / STATUS

Do not claim reminders eliminate no-shows.

# 22. THE REACTIVATION WAREHOUSE

Hypothetical visual:

CRM DATABASE

[old leads]
[unsold estimates]
[former customers]
[inactive patients]
[expired opportunities]

Then ask:

> How much customer-acquisition effort is sitting inside this database without a current next step?

Do not say the entire database is recoverable.

A reactivation scenario should move through:

->
CONTACTABLE
->
RESPONSE
->
QUALIFIED
->

# 23. THE REPORTING STORY - DRIVING THROUGH THE REARVIEW MIRROR

Use carefully; do not overdo the metaphor.

> Imagine management only learns what happened last week after three employees spend Monday morning assembling spreadsheets. The data may be accurate, but leadership is making decisions after the fact.

Ask:

> What report takes your team the longest to assemble?

Then visualize:

SYSTEM A + SYSTEM B + SYSTEM C
->
MANUAL SPREADSHEET
->
MANAGER

versus:

CONNECTED DATA
->
DASHBOARD
->
MANAGER

AI may summarize the information, but the underlying data must be reliable.

# 24. THE HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP VISUAL

Some owners hear automation and assume:

AI decides everything.

Show instead:

ROUTINE INPUT
->
AI / AUTOMATION ASSISTS
->
RULE CHECK
->
HUMAN REVIEW WHERE NEEDED
->
ACTION

Examples where human review may be necessary:

- legal decisions
- medical decisions
- financial approvals
- hiring / firing
- safety-critical work
- compliance
- significant commitments
- sensitive customer decisions

This visual helps sell safety, not just automation.

# 25. THE THREE-COLUMN WORKFLOW VISUAL

A powerful discovery-page layout for the final PDF:

| TRIGGER | CURRENT PROCESS | POSSIBLE IMPROVED PROCESS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New lead | Manual callback | Immediate acknowledgment + routing |
| Missed call | Voicemail | Capture + callback workflow |
| Unsold estimate | Rep remembers | Structured sequence + salesperson task |
| Report needed | Spreadsheet assembly | Connected reporting |
| Repetitive intake | Employee retypes | Structured capture + human review |

Important:

The third column is a possibility.

It is not a promised implementation before technical discovery.

# 26. THE OWNER SHOULD DRAW WITH YOU

Whenever practical, let the owner help map the process.

Ask:

> What happens next?

Then draw the next box.

> Who owns that step?

Write the role.

> Where does it usually get stuck?

Mark the leak.

> What system is involved?

Write the system name.

This turns the sales conversation into collaborative diagnosis.

The owner is no longer being presented to.

They are helping expose the workflow.

---

# 27. WHITEBOARD / NAPKIN SELLING

A rep does not always need slides.

The simplest field visualization:

LEAD
->
RESPONSE
->
FOLLOW-UP
->
SALE

Then circle where the prospect says the process breaks.

Write the numbers underneath.

Example:

300 leads
->
270 contacted
->
180 qualified
->
90 appointments
->
45 customers

Only use numbers provided or clearly labeled as scenario assumptions.

The visual should make complexity smaller.

Not make the salesperson look clever.

---

# 28. PHONE VISUALIZATION WITHOUT A VISUAL

On the phone, use verbal signposts.

> Let me make sure I'm picturing this correctly. The lead comes in, it goes to the office, somebody manually assigns it, the salesperson calls once, and if they don't answer there isn't a standard second step. Is that accurate?

The prospect will correct you.

That correction improves discovery.

Then:

> So the leak isn't necessarily lead generation. It's between first contact and follow-up.

That is verbal visualization.

---

# 29. IN-PERSON VISUALIZATION

In person, use:

- one-page field card
- tablet
- notepad
- ROI worksheet
- simple process diagram

Do not open a 60-slide presentation during a cold walk-in.

If the owner gives you three minutes, draw three boxes.

If the owner gives you a scheduled hour, use the more complete visualization.

Match the visual to the permission level.

---

# 30. THE PAIN -> PICTURE -> NUMBER -> NEXT STEP FRAMEWORK

Use:

## PAIN

> You said calls hit voicemail after hours.

## PICTURE

> So a customer who needs service tonight may call the next company before your team sees the voicemail.

## NUMBER

> Roughly how many of those calls do you receive?

## NEXT STEP

> Let's determine whether the volume and customer value are large enough to justify improving the workflow.

This is the YAD version of visualization selling.

---

# 31. NEVER USE FEAR WITHOUT A FACTUAL BASE

Do not say:

> You're probably losing hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Do not say:

> Your competitors are taking all of these customers.

Do not say:

> This will put you out of business.

Instead:

> That creates an exposure worth measuring.

> Let's use your numbers.

> We need to determine how much is realistically recoverable.

The story creates relevance.

The numbers create credibility.

---

# 32. NEVER HUMILIATE THE EMPLOYEE IN THE STORY

Bad story:

> Your receptionist forgets to follow up and loses the lead.

Better:

> The receptionist is handling customers, calls, scheduling, and administrative work simultaneously. The current system depends on one person remembering the next step while juggling everything else.

Now the problem is the process.

The employee can become a champion of the improvement.

---

# 33. NEVER MAKE AI THE HERO

Bad:

> Then our AI agent comes in and handles everything.

Better:

> The routine steps can happen consistently, the CRM can stay updated, and the employee can focus on the parts that require judgment, relationships, and exceptions.

The hero is:

A better business system.

Not AI.

---

# 34. THE VISUAL EVIDENCE LADDER

When presenting a diagram or story, label what each element represents.

## VERIFIED CLIENT DATA

Actual numbers supplied / verified by prospect.

## EXTERNAL BENCHMARK

Credible research with source and limitations.

## YAD PLANNING ASSUMPTION

A clearly identified scenario assumption.

## HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO

Illustrative example only.

Do not mix the categories visually without labels.

# 36. INDUSTRY STORY CARD FORMAT

Every final vertical playbook should contain at least three stories.

## STORY 1 - REVENUE / CUSTOMER OPPORTUNITY

Example: missed call, slow lead, unsold estimate.

## STORY 2 - EMPLOYEE CAPACITY / OPERATIONS

Example: repetitive administration, reporting, duplicate entry.

## STORY 3 - CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE / VISIBILITY

Example: status updates, intake, scheduling, attribution.

Each story card contains:

- title
- REAL VERIFIED or HYPOTHETICAL label
- 15-second version
- 45-second version
- visual diagram
- discovery question
- financial question
- appropriate YAD capability category
- what NOT to claim

# 37. STORY DRILL - HVAC

Manager says:

> HVAC company. Google Ads. Calls go to voicemail after 6 PM.

Rep must deliver:

1. 15-second story.
2. Relevance question.
3. Frequency question.
4. Value question.
5. Better-workflow visual.
6. Claim guardrail.

Pass example:

> Hypothetically, a homeowner clicks your paid ad at 9 PM, calls, gets voicemail, and calls the next company. What happens to that call here today? Roughly how many after-hours opportunities do you see in a month? I'm not assuming they're all customers - we'd need your numbers before putting value on it.

---

# 38. STORY DRILL - EMPLOYEE CAPACITY

Manager says:

> Five-person office team. Heavy repetitive data entry.

Rep:

> Imagine your best coordinator spending several hours every week moving the same information between systems. The employee isn't the problem. The question is whether the workflow requires that person's attention every time. If we could safely remove part of that repetitive load, what higher-value work would you want those hours redirected toward?

Pass if the rep does NOT say:

> We can eliminate one of the five employees.

->

->
WHAT HAPPENED?
->
WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?

# 40. STORY CERTIFICATION SCORECARD

Managers should grade:

1. Story is relevant to the prospect.
2. Story is short enough for the setting.
3. Hypothetical examples are labeled honestly.
4. No invented case studies.
5. No invented numbers.
6. Story focuses on workflow, not AI hype.
7. Employees are not blamed.
8. Human judgment is preserved where needed.
9. Story ends in a discovery question.
10. Rep moves from story to prospect data.
11. Rep distinguishes exposure from recoverable value.
12. Visual is simple and understandable.
13. Rep does not overpromise implementation.
14. Next step is appropriate.

---

# MODULE 6 PASS STANDARD

A salesperson passes when they can:

- turn an abstract workflow issue into a simple business story
- distinguish a verified case study from a hypothetical scenario
- tell the story in 15-second, 45-second, and 2-minute versions
- draw a simple current-state workflow
- draw a possible improved-state workflow
- explain CRM visually
- explain employee capacity without replacement language
- explain a revenue leak without claiming all exposure is recoverable
- use stories by phone and in person
- transition from story to the prospect's own numbers
- select the appropriate ROI worksheet
- keep human oversight in sensitive workflows
- avoid making AI the hero

The rep should be able to make the owner see the business process clearly enough that the next question becomes obvious:

> How often is that happening here, and what is it worth?

That is the purpose of Story and Visualization Selling.

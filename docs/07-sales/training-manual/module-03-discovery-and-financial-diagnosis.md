# Module 3 - Discovery and Financial Diagnosis

Status: Active Working Draft

## Purpose

Discovery is where the salesperson learns how the business actually works before recommending technology.

The objective is not to ask every possible question. The objective is to uncover enough information to understand:

- where money enters the business
- where opportunities are being lost
- where employees lose time
- where customers experience friction
- where systems are disconnected
- where management lacks visibility
- which problems are financially important enough to solve
- which problems should remain human-led
- which opportunities require deeper assessment or technical review

The salesperson should leave discovery able to explain the business problem back to the prospect more clearly than the prospect described it initially.

---

# 1. Discovery is not interrogation

Do not run down a checklist mechanically.

A good discovery conversation feels like:

Question -> Answer -> Follow-up -> Quantification -> Confirmation

Example:

"What happens when a new lead comes in?"

"The office gets an email."

"Who normally sees it first?"

"Usually Ashley."

"What happens if Ashley is with a customer?"

"It may sit there."

"How long can it sit?"

"Sometimes an hour or two."

"And roughly how many inbound leads do you get in a month?"

Now the rep has found a workflow, a failure point, and a number worth investigating.

---

# 2. The seven discovery layers

Every meaningful YAD discovery should explore the following layers when relevant.

## Layer 1 - Business model

Understand how the company makes money.

Ask:
- What are your main revenue lines?
- What is a typical customer/job/case/account worth?
- Is the business transactional, recurring, project-based, or a mix?
- What products/services are most profitable or strategically important?
- How long is the typical sales cycle?
- Is demand seasonal or event-driven?

## Layer 2 - Lead generation and marketing

Understand how opportunities enter the company.

Ask:
- Where do your best leads come from?
- What paid channels are you using?
- Roughly what do you spend monthly?
- Can you track source to customer and revenue?
- Which channels produce the best customers, not merely the most leads?
- Do you use dedicated landing pages and call tracking?

## Layer 3 - Sales and follow-up

Understand how an opportunity becomes a customer.

Ask:
- What happens after a lead arrives?
- How is it assigned?
- How fast is the first response?
- What happens after the first unanswered attempt?
- Do you use a CRM?
- Is CRM adoption consistent?
- Do you know your lead-to-customer conversion rate?
- What happens to unsold estimates/proposals?
- How long does follow-up continue?
- Do old leads ever get reactivated?

## Layer 4 - Customer service and communication

Understand how customers interact with the business after contact.

Ask:
- How many calls come in?
- What percentage go unanswered?
- What happens after hours?
- How are appointments scheduled?
- How are reminders handled?
- What questions do customers ask repeatedly?
- What causes the most complaints or status calls?
- Where should a human always remain involved?

## Layer 5 - Operations and employee workload

Understand where staff time goes.

Ask:
- What repetitive work consumes the most employee time?
- What gets entered more than once?
- Where do employees copy information between systems?
- What work routinely creates overtime?
- Where is the team overwhelmed?
- What breaks when volume increases?
- If the company grew 50%, where would you need to add administrative capacity first?

Permanent rule: this conversation is about improving capacity and workflows, not replacing employees.

## Layer 6 - Technology, data, and reporting

Understand the system environment.

Ask:
- What are the major software systems?
- Which systems talk to each other?
- Where are spreadsheets still required?
- What reports are assembled manually?
- Which KPIs can leadership see easily?
- Which numbers does management have to guess?
- Are important SOPs and knowledge easy for employees to access?

## Layer 7 - Strategy, urgency, and buying process

Understand whether the problem can become a real engagement.

Ask:
- What are the company's top priorities this year?
- Which problem would leadership fix first if it could?
- What happens if nothing changes for 6-12 months?
- Who besides you would evaluate a project like this?
- What would need to be true for the company to move forward?
- How do you normally approve technology or consulting investments?

---

# 3. Follow the money

The rep should constantly connect process questions to economics.

When the prospect says:

"We miss some calls."

Ask:
- How many calls?
- How many are new business?
- What is an average customer worth?
- What happens to those calls today?

When the prospect says:

"Our office is buried."

Ask:
- Which tasks consume the time?
- How many employees?
- Roughly how many hours?
- Does it cause overtime, backlog, slower customer response, or planned hiring?

When the prospect says:

"Our ads suck."

Ask:
- What are you spending?
- What are you tracking?
- How many qualified leads?
- How many customers?
- Do you know acquisition cost by channel?
- Is the issue traffic, conversion, lead quality, response, sales, or attribution?

---

# 4. The workflow map

For an important process, map:

Trigger -> Input -> Person/System -> Action -> Handoff -> Decision -> Outcome -> Measurement

Example:

Website lead -> Form submission -> Email inbox -> Receptionist -> CRM entry -> Salesperson -> Phone call -> Appointment -> Estimate -> Follow-up -> Sale/Lost -> Revenue reporting

At each arrow ask:

- What happens here?
- Who owns it?
- Which software is involved?
- How long does it take?
- What can go wrong?
- How often does it go wrong?
- Is it measured?

This is where high-value YAD opportunities often appear.

---

# 5. The leak ladder

When a problem is found, move down this ladder:

1. Symptom - what is happening?
2. Frequency - how often?
3. Cause - why?
4. Current workaround - what do people do today?
5. Cost - what does this represent financially or operationally?
6. Desired state - what would good look like?
7. Constraints - what must remain human, compliant, secure, or approved?
8. Feasibility - what needs technical review?
9. Priority - is it worth solving now?

---

# 6. Quantification questions

Reps should develop the habit of asking:

- How many per day/week/month?
- What percentage?
- How long does each one take?
- How many employees touch it?
- What is one customer/job/appointment worth?
- What does one hour of this role cost?
- How much do you spend to create the lead?
- What percentage do you believe is realistically recoverable?
- What would you consider a conservative improvement?

Do not ask all of them if they do not fit the problem.

---

# 7. Source every important number

For every major number, identify whether it is:

- verified client data
- client-provided estimate
- external benchmark
- illustrative assumption

A rep should never leave a discovery call with a spreadsheet full of numbers and no idea where those numbers came from.

---

# 8. Unknown is an acceptable answer

When the prospect does not know a number, write:

"Unknown - measure."

Examples:
- missed call rate unknown
- conversion unknown
- CAC unknown
- unsold estimate count unknown
- administrative hours unknown

Do not embarrass the owner.

Say:

"That's useful. I don't want to invent a number. That's one of the things we'd want to measure before putting a strong ROI claim around it."

Lack of visibility may itself be an opportunity.

---

# 9. The five business leaks during discovery

Tag discoveries as:

- Revenue Leakage
- Marketing Leakage
- Labor Leakage
- Capacity Leakage
- Information Leakage

A single problem can touch multiple categories.

Example:

Unanswered paid leads can create:
- Revenue Leakage - prospective customers lost
- Marketing Leakage - paid acquisition not converted
- Capacity Leakage - office cannot handle inbound volume
- Information Leakage - no call tracking or attribution

This helps the salesperson see the complete opportunity rather than pitching one isolated tool.

---

# 10. Pain versus priority

Not every pain is a priority.

A prospect may hate a task that only costs $2,000 per year.

Another problem they barely notice may represent a six-figure opportunity.

Ask:

"If we could only fix one of these this quarter, which would matter most?"

Then compare emotional priority with economic priority.

---

# 11. Discovery red flags

Slow down when:

- nobody can explain the workflow
- each employee describes the process differently
- the CRM data is unreliable
- prospect wants a guarantee before sharing data
- prospect expects AI to make sensitive decisions without review
- prospect wants automation primarily to terminate staff
- requested integration has not been technically verified
- the business lacks enough volume to justify custom development
- leadership is unwilling to change the process
- the project has no owner internally

These do not automatically kill a deal, but they change how it should be scoped.

---

# 12. Employee-safe discovery language

Say:

"Which parts of this process are frustrating or repetitive for your team?"

"Where are good employees spending time on work that doesn't need their judgment?"

"If we gave the team five hours back every week, where would those hours create the most value?"

"What becomes overloaded when business increases?"

Avoid:

"Whose job can we automate?"

"How many employees can we replace?"

"Who could you get rid of?"

---

# 13. Human-review discovery

Ask explicitly:

- Which decisions must stay with a person?
- What requires professional judgment?
- What customer situations should always escalate to staff?
- What approvals are required?
- What information is sensitive?
- What actions create legal, financial, medical, safety, employment, or compliance consequences?

The system should be designed around those constraints rather than discovered after implementation.

---

# 14. Discovery conversation structure

## Opening

"I want to understand how the business works before talking about what we would build. If there's no meaningful opportunity, I'd rather know that than force an AI project."

## Business

Understand revenue model, customers, priorities, and growth.

## Workflow

Pick the most important customer, sales, operational, or administrative process and map it.

## Leakage

Identify failure points and repeat work.

## Economics

Use the appropriate Module 2B calculator.

## Desired state

Ask what better would look like.

## Constraints

Identify people, technical, security, compliance, and process requirements.

## Next step

Determine whether the correct move is:
- more data collection
- assessment
- executive strategy
- marketing audit
- workflow analysis
- technical feasibility review
- implementation proposal
- no project yet

---

# 15. The discovery recap

Before ending, summarize:

"Let me make sure I have this right..."

Then explain:

1. Current workflow
2. Where it breaks
3. How often
4. What the prospect believes it is worth
5. What is still unknown
6. What a better state would look like
7. The next logical step

Ask:

"Did I miss anything or misunderstand any part of that?"

A strong recap proves the salesperson listened and gives the prospect a chance to correct the model.

---

# 16. Opportunity statement

After discovery, the rep should be able to write one paragraph:

"[Company] currently handles approximately [volume] through [workflow]. The process appears to break at [failure point], affecting approximately [frequency/percentage]. Based on [source of inputs], this represents [theoretical exposure / capacity] and a conservative [recoverable / addressable] scenario of approximately [value]. The main unknowns are [missing data]. A potential next step is [assessment/strategy/audit/technical review] to validate the economics and determine the simplest reliable solution."

This is not a proposal. It is a diagnosis summary.

---

# 17. Qualification after discovery

A strong opportunity usually has several of these characteristics:

- meaningful business value
- recurring/high-volume problem
- measurable baseline
- identifiable workflow owner
- technical access appears plausible
- leadership urgency
- willingness to share data
- willingness to change process
- reasonable investment capacity
- opportunity can be measured after implementation

A weak opportunity may have:

- low frequency
- low customer value
- unclear owner
- no willingness to measure
- unrealistic expectations
- no process discipline
- no budget or urgency
- excessive implementation risk relative to value

---

# 18. Discovery scoring card

After the conversation, score 1-5:

- Economic Value
- Frequency / Volume
- Data Quality
- Urgency
- Decision-Maker Access
- Implementation Feasibility
- Measurability
- Expansion Potential

Do not expose the internal score to the prospect automatically.

The score helps reps prioritize follow-up and escalate the best opportunities.

---

# 19. What the rep should bring into discovery

- YAD Field Playbook
- appropriate ROI forms
- industry field card when available
- note-taking method / CRM
- calculator
- basic understanding of prospect's website, reviews, ads, locations, and services

The salesperson should research enough to ask better questions, not enough to pretend they already know the business.

---

# 20. Module 3 certification

A rep passes when they can:

- open discovery without pitching technology
- map a real workflow
- identify at least one business leak
- quantify the problem with defensible inputs
- distinguish verified data from estimates/assumptions
- identify missing data instead of inventing it
- use employee-safe capacity language
- identify required human review
- choose the correct ROI worksheet
- summarize the diagnosis clearly
- recommend the next logical commitment without forcing a sale

## Final rule

The goal of discovery is not to find something to sell.

The goal is to understand the business well enough to know what is worth solving.

# MODULE 9 - PROPOSAL, PRESENTATION, AND CLOSING

Status: Working Draft - Internal Sales Training

## Purpose

A proposal should not be the first time the prospect understands the problem, the economics, or the recommended next step.

By the time Your AI Department presents a proposal, the prospect should already understand:

- what is happening today
- why it matters
- where the workflow breaks
- what the problem may be worth
- what should remain human
- what type of improvement is being recommended
- why the proposed next step is appropriate

The proposal then documents the recommendation, scope, assumptions, investment, responsibilities, success measures, and next decision.

The objective is not to create the longest proposal.

The objective is to make a responsible business decision easy to understand.

The core sequence is:

DISCOVERY
->
FINANCIAL DIAGNOSIS
->
RECOMMENDATION
->
FUTURE-STATE WORKFLOW
->
BUSINESS CASE
->
SCOPE
->
INVESTMENT
->
DECISION
->
HANDOFF

---

# 1. CLOSING BEGINS DURING DISCOVERY

Closing is not a magical sentence at the end of a presentation.

A strong close is usually the natural result of the prospect agreeing that:

1. A meaningful problem exists.
2. The problem is important enough to address.
3. The proposed approach is credible.
4. The economics justify the next step.
5. The organization is capable of implementing the change.
6. The right people are involved in the decision.

If those conditions do not exist, aggressive closing language will not fix the deal.

The salesperson should continuously confirm understanding during discovery.

Examples:

> So the issue is not that you lack leads. It is that the follow-up becomes inconsistent after the first attempt. Is that accurate?

> If the numbers show this is materially affecting revenue, would fixing it be a priority?

> If we can preserve the human relationship while making the routine workflow more consistent, is that the direction you would want to explore?

These are not tricks.

They are alignment checks.

---

# 2. DO NOT PROPOSE BEFORE YOU UNDERSTAND THE PROBLEM

Do not send a complex implementation proposal because a prospect says:

> We want AI.

You may still need:

- workflow discovery
- system inventory
- integration review
- data review
- security / compliance review
- decision-maker alignment
- baseline metrics
- employee impact analysis
- technical feasibility

If the problem is not sufficiently defined, the correct commercial next step may be:

- additional discovery
- AI Department Assessment
- strategy conversation
- Executive AI Strategy
- technical feasibility review
- marketing audit
- CRM/workflow review

Do not turn uncertainty into a fabricated implementation scope.

---

# 3. MATCH THE SALE TO THE LEVEL OF CERTAINTY

Use three broad levels.

## LEVEL 1 - PROBLEM IDENTIFIED, SOLUTION NOT FULLY DEFINED

Possible next step:

Executive AI Strategy or deeper discovery.

Use when:

- multiple departments are involved
- several opportunities exist
- technology environment is complex
- leadership needs prioritization
- build-vs-buy is unclear
- ROI requires more data
- governance / security needs analysis

## LEVEL 2 - DEFINED PROBLEM, FEASIBLE IMPLEMENTATION PATH

Possible next step:

AI Implementation proposal.

Use when:

- workflow is sufficiently understood
- technical requirements are reasonably known
- desired outcome is defined
- implementation responsibilities are understood
- measurement is possible

## LEVEL 3 - MULTIPLE ONGOING OPPORTUNITIES

Possible next step:

Managed AI Department.

Use when:

- leadership needs ongoing AI ownership
- multiple departments / projects exist
- implementation will continue over time
- optimization and training are recurring needs
- the company wants an ongoing partner

Do not force every prospect through every level.

# 4. CURRENT COMMERCIAL TRUTH

Salespeople must use current approved commercial information.

## EXECUTIVE AI STRATEGY

Canonical paid strategy offer.

Indicative starting point:

Approximately $5,000+

Final pricing depends on factors such as:

- company size
- number of departments
- number of locations
- complexity
- discovery requirements
- technology environment
- required deliverables

## AI IMPLEMENTATION

Indicative project range:

Approximately $5,000-$50,000+

More complex implementations may exceed this range.

Final scope and pricing depend on actual requirements.

## MANAGED AI DEPARTMENT

Flagship ongoing relationship.

Pricing is not yet finalized in the current repository.

Do NOT invent Starter / Professional / Enterprise prices.

## ASSESSMENT / SCHEDULING

Use the current approved assessment and scheduling process at the time of sale.

Do not hard-code conflicting legacy pricing or scheduling links into scripts.

# 5. THE PROPOSAL SHOULD TELL ONE BUSINESS STORY

A proposal should answer:

## WHERE ARE YOU TODAY?

Current workflow and problem.

## WHY DOES IT MATTER?

Business impact.

## WHAT SHOULD CHANGE?

Recommended future state.

## HOW WILL WE HELP?

Scope.

## WHAT WILL THE CLIENT NEED TO DO?

Responsibilities and dependencies.

## HOW WILL WE KNOW IT WORKED?

Success metrics.

## WHAT DOES IT COST?

Investment.

## WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?

Decision and kickoff path.

If the proposal does not clearly answer those questions, it is not ready.

---

# 6. RECOMMENDED PROPOSAL STRUCTURE

For significant strategy or implementation proposals, use a structure similar to:

1. Executive Summary
2. Current-State Finding
3. Business Impact / Opportunity
4. Objectives
5. Recommended Future-State Workflow
6. Scope of Work
7. Deliverables
8. Human Responsibilities / Human Review
9. Technical Assumptions and Dependencies
10. Success Metrics / Baseline
11. Implementation Phases or Approach
12. Investment
13. Exclusions / Out of Scope
14. Client Responsibilities
15. Next Steps / Approval

The exact document can be shorter for simple engagements.

Do not add sections simply to make the proposal look larger.

---

# 7. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The executive summary should be short.

It should not say:

> YAD is a revolutionary AI company leveraging cutting-edge artificial intelligence.

It should say what is happening in the client's business.

Example:

> Based on discovery, the company currently generates meaningful inbound demand through paid search, but after-hours calls and unanswered inquiries do not consistently enter a measurable follow-up workflow. YAD recommends a phased lead-response and CRM workflow designed to capture, route, track, and follow up appropriate opportunities while keeping employee judgment in the customer relationship.

That is client-centered.

---

# 8. PRESENT THE CURRENT STATE FIRST

Before showing the recommendation, make sure the prospect agrees with the current-state diagnosis.

Example:

CURRENT STATE

Paid Ad
->
Phone Call
->
Office Busy / Closed
->
Voicemail
->
Manual Callback
->
Outcome Not Consistently Tracked

Ask:

> Is this an accurate picture of what happens today?

If they say no:

Fix the diagram.

Do not present a solution to a workflow they do not recognize.

---

# 9. PRESENT THE BUSINESS IMPACT SECOND

Use the prospect's own numbers where possible.

Separate:

## THEORETICAL EXPOSURE

What value is represented by the problem.

## REALISTICALLY RECOVERABLE OPPORTUNITY

What portion may plausibly improve under an explicit scenario.

## POTENTIAL ROI

What the scenario may mean relative to the investment.

Never say:

> This project will make you $180,000.

Use:

> Using the assumptions we reviewed together, the expected scenario represents approximately $180,000 of annual potential value. That is not guaranteed revenue; it is the business case we would measure against.

Every assumption should be labeled.

---

# 10. THE THREE-NUMBER PRESENTATION

When possible, simplify the financial discussion to three numbers.

Example:

CURRENT ANNUAL EXPOSURE
$240,000

EXPECTED RECOVERABLE VALUE SCENARIO
$72,000

FIRST-YEAR INVESTMENT
$24,000

Then discuss:

Potential net value.

Simple ROI.

Payback.

The rep should be able to explain every number.

If they cannot explain where a number came from, it should not be in the proposal.

---

# 11. USE RANGE-BASED SCENARIOS WHEN UNCERTAINTY EXISTS

For uncertain outcomes, present:

CONSERVATIVE

EXPECTED

AGGRESSIVE

The expected case should not secretly be the aggressive case.

The conservative case should still use reasonable assumptions.

Example:

Conservative capture improvement: 10%

Expected: 20%

Aggressive: 30%

Only use percentages that have a defensible basis or are explicitly labeled planning assumptions.

Do not reverse-engineer the assumptions until the ROI looks attractive.

---

# 12. PRESENT THE FUTURE STATE VISUALLY

After the prospect confirms the current state, show the possible improved state.

Example:

CURRENT

Lead
->
Email Notification
->
Salesperson Busy
->
Delay
->
Manual Follow-Up
->
Unknown Outcome

POSSIBLE FUTURE STATE

Lead
->
CRM Record
->
Source Captured
->
Immediate Acknowledgment
->
Assigned Owner
->
Human / AI-Assisted Qualification Where Appropriate
->
Appointment / Next Step
->
Follow-Up
->
Tracked Outcome

Say:

> This is the workflow we are proposing to design and implement. The exact technology and integrations still have to match the systems we validate.

---

# 13. DO NOT SELL A FEATURE LIST

Weak proposal:

- AI agent
- Zapier
- API
- chatbot
- GPT
- dashboard
- SMS
- webhook

Stronger proposal:

## OBJECTIVE

Reduce the number of qualified inbound opportunities that disappear without a defined next step.

## WORKFLOW

Capture -> Route -> Respond -> Follow Up -> Track -> Report

## TECHNOLOGY

Selected and implemented based on compatibility and requirements.

Business outcome first.

Technology second.

---

# 14. SCOPE SHOULD BE SPECIFIC ENOUGH TO CONTROL EXPECTATIONS

A scope should define what YAD is responsible for.

Example:

Potential scope:

- map current inbound-lead workflow
- configure CRM pipeline
- connect approved website lead sources where feasible
- create lead-assignment rules
- configure approved SMS/email follow-up workflows
- create missed-call recovery workflow where technically supported
- implement dashboard for defined KPIs
- test workflows
- train designated users
- document operating procedure

Do not write:

> Automate sales.

That is too vague.

---

# 15. DEFINE WHAT IS OUT OF SCOPE

Good proposals protect both sides.

Potential exclusions may include:

- unapproved custom software
- unsupported integrations
- data cleansing beyond defined scope
- historical data migration beyond agreed volume
- legal / compliance advice
- replacement of client IT security responsibilities
- advertising spend
- third-party SaaS fees unless explicitly included
- SMS / phone / API usage fees unless explicitly included
- new creative production unless included
- additional locations / departments outside scope

The exact exclusions depend on the project.

Do not use this list mechanically.

---

# 16. THIRD-PARTY COSTS MUST BE CLEAR

A YAD project may involve:

- CRM subscription
- phone platform
- AI model usage
- SMS usage
- email platform
- automation platform
- call tracking
- advertising spend
- software licenses
- API usage

Do not create confusion about whether those costs are included.

The proposal should identify third-party costs when known and specify whether they are:

- included
- billed separately
- client-paid
- estimated
- not yet determined

Never hide recurring third-party costs to make the project price look smaller.

---

# 17. CLIENT RESPONSIBILITIES MATTER

Implementation may require the client to provide:

- system access
- decision-maker availability
- workflow subject-matter experts
- data
- API / vendor access
- employee participation
- testing feedback
- approval of messaging
- security / compliance requirements
- timely decisions

If the client does not participate, implementation may stall.

A proposal should make responsibilities visible.

---

# 18. DEFINE HUMAN REVIEW IN THE PROPOSAL

Where relevant, explicitly state what remains human.

Examples:

AI drafts -> employee approves.

AI collects preliminary information -> professional reviews.

Automation routes the lead -> salesperson closes.

AI summarizes the call -> manager decides.

Routine reminders automate -> employee handles exceptions.

For legal, medical, financial, employment, compliance, safety, or other sensitive workflows, appropriate human oversight should be explicit.

---

# 19. DEFINE SUCCESS BEFORE IMPLEMENTATION

Do not wait until after launch to decide whether the project worked.

Potential KPIs:

- lead response time
- missed-call recovery rate
- percentage of leads entering CRM
- follow-up compliance
- appointment rate
- estimate follow-up rate
- conversion rate
- processing time
- repetitive hours reduced
- employee adoption
- no-show rate
- reporting time
- attribution coverage
- pipeline visibility

Not every project requires every KPI.

Choose the metrics tied directly to the business case.

---

# 20. BASELINE BEFORE CLAIMING IMPROVEMENT

If the client does not know the current metric, do not invent the baseline.

Use:

> Baseline measurement required during initial implementation phase.

A project may need to begin by measuring:

- current response time
- missed-call volume
- follow-up rate
- employee hours
- conversion
- no-shows
- reporting time

Without a baseline, improvement claims become weak.

---

# 21. TIMELINE DISCIPLINE

Do not promise:

> We can have the whole thing live by Friday.

before scope and technical dependencies are validated.

Use phases rather than unsupported dates when necessary.

Possible phases:

1. Discovery / Validation
2. Design
3. Configuration / Build
4. Integration
5. Testing
6. Training
7. Deployment
8. Measurement / Optimization

Specific dates and durations should be approved based on actual project scope.

---

# 22. PILOT BEFORE SCALE

A pilot may be appropriate when:

- the workflow is new
- AI reliability must be validated
- customer reaction is uncertain
- integrations are complex
- employee adoption is uncertain
- the economics are promising but not proven
- the company has many locations / departments

The pilot should have:

- defined workflow
- defined users / location
- success metrics
- test period or milestone
- decision rule for expansion

The salesperson should not treat a pilot as a fake discount.

It is a risk-management tool.

---

# 23. PRESENTATION ORDER

Recommended presentation flow:

## 1. RECAP WHAT WE HEARD

> Here's what we heard from your team.

## 2. CONFIRM CURRENT STATE

Show workflow.

## 3. SHOW THE BUSINESS IMPACT

Use their numbers.

## 4. SHOW THE DESIRED FUTURE STATE

Keep it visual.

## 5. EXPLAIN THE RECOMMENDED NEXT STEP

Strategy / implementation / managed relationship.

## 6. REVIEW SCOPE

What YAD will do.

## 7. REVIEW RESPONSIBILITIES / ASSUMPTIONS

What must be true.

## 8. REVIEW SUCCESS METRICS

How results will be measured.

## 9. PRESENT INVESTMENT

Clear and calm.

## 10. ASK FOR THE DECISION OR NEXT COMMITMENT

Then stop talking.

---

# 24. DO NOT APOLOGIZE FOR THE PRICE

Bad:

> So unfortunately it's going to be $18,500...

Better:

> The investment for the scope we just reviewed is $18,500.

Then stop.

Allow the prospect to process it.

Do not immediately begin discounting your own price before they object.

---

# 25. PRICE MUST FOLLOW VALUE AND SCOPE

Do not open a presentation with:

> This is $25,000.

unless the prospect explicitly asks for price first.

The normal sequence is:

Problem
->
Impact
->
Future State
->
Scope
->
Investment

The price should make sense in the context of the business case.

This does not mean hiding the price.

It means presenting it in the correct context.

---

# 26. WHEN THEY ASK FOR PRICE EARLY

Do not evade.

Use current approved ranges when appropriate.

Example:

> Implementation work can vary dramatically. Our current indicative range for many projects is roughly $5,000 to $50,000+, but I do not want to pretend your project is a $10,000 project or a $40,000 project before we understand the workflow and integrations.

For Executive AI Strategy:

> The current indicative starting point is around $5,000+, with final pricing depending on the size and complexity of the company and the required deliverables.

Do not invent Managed AI Department package pricing.

---

# 27. THE EXECUTIVE AI STRATEGY CLOSE

Use when the company has meaningful opportunity but still needs prioritization.

Example:

> You have enough opportunity across lead handling, CRM, employee capacity, reporting, and AI adoption that I do not think we should jump straight into buying technology. The more responsible next step is to map the major workflows, quantify the opportunities, decide what should remain human, prioritize the highest-value initiatives, and build the implementation roadmap. That is what the Executive AI Strategy engagement is designed to do.

Then:

> Based on what we've reviewed, are you comfortable moving forward with the strategy engagement?

Do not oversell it as a workshop.

---

# 28. THE IMPLEMENTATION CLOSE

Use when the problem and solution path are sufficiently defined.

Example:

> We agree the primary issue is qualified inbound leads entering inconsistently, slow follow-up, and weak attribution. The proposed scope creates the CRM and response workflow, connects the approved lead sources, trains the team, and measures the results. If the scope and investment make sense, the next step is approval so we can move into technical validation and kickoff.

Then:

> Is there anything unresolved that would stop us from moving forward?

If no:

Proceed through the approved contracting / payment process.

---

# 29. THE MANAGED AI DEPARTMENT CLOSE

Use when ongoing ownership is the actual need.

Do not force a retainer because recurring revenue is attractive.

Example:

> What we're seeing is not one isolated automation. You have opportunities across multiple departments, and those opportunities will continue changing as the business grows. The case for an ongoing relationship would be continuous prioritization, implementation oversight, employee adoption, measurement, and new opportunity discovery rather than a collection of disconnected one-off projects.

Because current package pricing is not finalized, reps must use approved custom pricing only.

---

# 30. THE NEXT-STEP CLOSE

Not every conversation should close revenue.

A cold call may close for:

- discovery meeting
- assessment
- strategy conversation
- owner introduction
- technical review
- data request
- proposal review

Example:

> The next logical step is to get the owner and operations manager together for 30 minutes and map the actual workflow. Does Tuesday or Wednesday make more sense?

The close is the next justified commitment.

---

# 31. THE FIELD-SALES CLOSE

A walk-in rep should not attempt to price a complex AI transformation in a lobby.

Possible field closes:

> This sounds worth looking at with the actual numbers. Let's schedule a proper conversation with whoever owns operations and marketing.

> You've got the missed-call data in your phone system. If you can pull that, we can run the one-page ROI worksheet together on the next conversation.

> I do not want to guess at the integration while standing here. Let me get our technical side involved and come back with the right questions.

For a simple approved offer, a field rep may close directly if commercial authority and scope are clear.

Otherwise:

Close for the next step.

---

# 32. NEVER EMAIL A MAJOR PROPOSAL AND HOPE

For significant proposals, schedule a live review when possible.

Do not simply send:

> Attached is our proposal. Let me know what you think.

A live review allows you to:

- confirm the diagnosis
- explain assumptions
- answer questions
- identify stakeholder concerns
- clarify scope
- discuss investment
- define the decision process

If they insist on receiving it first, send it and schedule a review.

---

# 33. KNOW THE DECISION PROCESS BEFORE THE PROPOSAL

Before presenting, understand:

- Who decides?
- Who signs?
- Who influences?
- Who owns the budget?
- Who will use the system?
- Does IT need approval?
- Does legal / compliance need review?
- Is there procurement?
- Is another vendor involved?
- Is there a budget cycle?

Do not discover after the presentation that:

> My partner needs to see this.

Ask earlier:

> Who besides you should be part of the decision so we do not make you relay the entire business case secondhand?

# 35. DO NOT LET THE CHAMPION SELL THE DEAL FOR YOU

If your contact says:

> Send me something and I'll show the owner.

Try:

> Absolutely. I'd also be happy to give the owner the ten-minute version directly so you do not have to become our salesperson. What does their calendar look like?

Make it easier for the internal champion.

# 36. CLOSING QUESTIONS THAT FIT YAD

Approved examples:

> Is there anything unresolved that would prevent you from moving forward?

> Does the proposed workflow solve the problem we agreed matters most?

> Do the economics justify the investment in your view?

> Who else needs to approve this?

> What would need to change in the proposal for it to accurately reflect what you want to accomplish?

> If we resolve the technical question, are you comfortable proceeding?

> Are you ready to move into the next step?

Avoid manipulative lines designed to corner the buyer.

---

# 37. USE CONDITIONAL CLOSES FOR REAL DEPENDENCIES

Example:

> If our technical review confirms the CRM integration can be done reliably within this scope, are you comfortable moving forward with the project?

This is appropriate because the condition is real.

Do not invent fake conditions or fake scarcity.

---

# 38. HANDLE LAST-MINUTE OBJECTIONS USING MODULE 7

When the prospect objects after seeing the proposal:

LISTEN
->
CLARIFY
->
ISOLATE
->
RESPOND
->
CONFIRM
->
NEXT STEP

Do not react to:

> That's more than I expected.

with:

> I can knock 20% off.

Ask:

> Is the concern the total investment, the timing, the scope, or that the value case isn't strong enough yet?

Then address the actual issue.

# 39. SCOPE REDUCTION IS DIFFERENT FROM DISCOUNTING

Sometimes a prospect cannot justify the full scope.

A legitimate option may be:

FULL PROJECT

vs

PRIORITY PHASE 1

Example:

Instead of implementing:

CRM + phone + marketing attribution + dashboards + reactivation

Start with:

Lead capture + response + CRM pipeline

That is a scope decision.

Not:

> Same work for less money.

Any price concession must follow YAD's approved authority rules once finalized.

---

# 40. DO NOT CREATE FAKE URGENCY

Do not say:

> This price disappears Friday.

unless there is an actual approved deadline.

Do not say:

> Your competitors are already doing this.

unless verified and relevant.

Do not say:

> Every day you wait costs $5,000.

unless a defensible cost-of-delay analysis supports the claim.

Real urgency can come from:

- busy season
- known lead leakage
- new location opening
- hiring decision
- software renewal
- marketing scale-up
- compliance deadline
- operational bottleneck

Use real timing.

---

# 41. COST OF DELAY

Cost-of-delay can be useful only when the underlying opportunity is defensible.

Example:

If a validated workflow represents an expected $12,000 per month of recoverable value, a delayed decision may represent continued exposure.

Say:

> If the expected scenario is accurate, waiting three months means the current workflow remains unchanged for another three months.

Do not say:

> You lose exactly $36,000 if you wait.

unless the claim is fully supportable.

---

# 42. WHAT HAPPENS AFTER YES

A signed deal is not the finish line.

The salesperson must create a clean handoff.

Minimum handoff information:

- executive sponsor
- operational owner
- problem statement
- current workflow
- desired outcome
- scope sold
- excluded scope
- commercial terms
- assumptions
- integrations discussed
- integrations verified vs unverified
- systems involved
- data considerations
- human-review requirements
- baseline metrics
- ROI assumptions
- success metrics
- stakeholder concerns
- deadlines / business timing
- promises made
- open questions

If the implementation team is surprised by what sales promised, the sales process failed.

---

# 43. PROMISE AUTHORITY

A salesperson may discuss approved:

- YAD methodology
- approved offers
- approved indicative price ranges
- documented capabilities
- documented example workflows
- approved case studies

A salesperson may NOT independently promise:

- unsupported integration
- guaranteed result
- exact implementation date without approval
- custom feature not scoped
- security / compliance certification not verified
- fixed pricing outside authority
- discount outside authority
- payment terms outside authority
- custom SLA
- exclusivity
- unlimited revisions / support
- legal or regulatory outcome

When uncertain:

> I need to verify that before I put it in the proposal.

---

# 44. SALES-TO-DELIVERY HANDOFF CALL

For meaningful implementations, conduct a formal handoff.

The salesperson should explain:

1. Why the client bought.
2. What problem matters most.
3. What business value was discussed.
4. What the client believes YAD will do.
5. What remains uncertain.
6. Who has authority.
7. What would make the client call the project a success.

Delivery should be able to challenge unclear or unsupported assumptions before work begins.

---

# 45. DO NOT DISAPPEAR AFTER THE SALE

Sales may remain involved during:

- kickoff
- major milestone check-ins
- expectation clarification
- expansion discovery
- case-study development

But sales should not bypass the implementation owner or make new promises directly to the client without alignment.

---

# 46. EXPANSION SHOULD COME FROM RESULTS AND NEW DIAGNOSIS

Do not upsell randomly.

After implementation, ask:

- Did the workflow improve?
- What did the data show?
- What new bottleneck appeared?
- What opportunity is now the highest priority?
- Is another department experiencing a similar issue?

Expansion should follow the same YAD doctrine:

DIAGNOSE
->
QUANTIFY
->
PRIORITIZE
->
RECOMMEND

---

# 47. CASE-STUDY CAPTURE SHOULD BEGIN AT KICKOFF

If appropriate and permitted, establish:

- baseline
- pre-project workflow
- initial pain
- target KPI
- implementation scope
- measurement method

Then review results at appropriate milestones such as 30 / 60 / 90 days when the engagement supports it.

Only verified, approved results can become public or rep-usable case studies.

This prevents future salespeople from inventing outcomes.

---

# 48. WHEN THE CORRECT CLOSE IS NO

Do not close a project when:

- the economics are weak
- the client refuses required human oversight
- technical feasibility fails
- security / compliance cannot be responsibly addressed
- adoption is impossible
- the client expects guaranteed financial outcomes
- the prospect wants unsupported functionality
- the prospect wants deceptive or unethical activity
- the simplest solution is something they can reasonably do without YAD

Say:

> Based on what we know, I don't think this scope is the right investment yet.

Trust compounds.

---

# 49. PROPOSAL QUALITY GATE

Before presenting, the rep or sales manager should be able to answer YES to:

- Is the business problem clearly defined?
- Did the prospect confirm the current workflow?
- Is the impact measurable or clearly labeled as requiring baseline measurement?
- Are ROI assumptions sourced and labeled?
- Is the proposed solution appropriate to the problem?
- Is human review defined where necessary?
- Has technical feasibility been verified enough for the promises being made?
- Is scope specific?
- Are exclusions clear?
- Are third-party costs clear?
- Are client responsibilities clear?
- Are success metrics defined?
- Is the investment approved?
- Are discounts / terms within authority?
- Are all decision-makers identified?
- Is the next step explicit?

If several answers are NO, the proposal is not ready.

# 53. ROLEPLAY - FIELD REP

Scenario:

Rep walks into a roofing company and discovers:

- several hundred unsold estimates
- each salesperson manages follow-up differently
- owner is interested but busy

Correct outcome:

Do NOT present a $25,000 implementation in the lobby.

Close for:

- owner + sales manager meeting
- CRM / estimate data
- follow-up workflow mapping
- ROI review

Then advance appropriately.

# 54. ROLEPLAY - CFO CHALLENGE

CFO asks:

> Why should I spend $30,000 on this if your ROI is not guaranteed?

Rep should respond:

> You should not spend $30,000 because of a guarantee from us. You should spend it only if the underlying problem, assumptions, implementation plan, and measurement threshold create a risk-adjusted business case you are comfortable with. We can make the assumptions explicit and define what would have to improve for the project to pay back.

Then walk through:

- baseline
- assumptions
- expected value
- investment
- payback threshold
- measurement

# 55. ROLEPLAY - SCOPE PRESSURE

Prospect:

> Can you add the website rebuild, all our SEO, a phone agent, a new CRM, three integrations, employee training, and dashboards for the same price?

Rep:

> Those are separate scope items. I do not want to say yes and then under-deliver. Let's separate what is necessary for the primary business outcome from what could be a later phase.

Then prioritize.

---

# 56. ROLEPLAY - DISCOUNT PRESSURE

Prospect:

> If you take 25% off, I'll sign today.

Rep does not invent authority.

Use:

> I can't authorize an unapproved price change on the spot. What I can do is understand whether the issue is budget or whether we should reduce the scope to the highest-priority phase.

Then follow approved commercial process.

# 57. ROLEPLAY - THE RIGHT ANSWER IS NO

Scenario:

Small business has:

- minimal lead volume
- no meaningful repetitive workflow
- low customer value
- no growth urgency
- wants a custom AI agent because it sounds impressive

Correct recommendation:

Do not sell the agent.

Potential answer:

> I don't think the economics justify custom implementation right now. You may get more value from a standard AI tool and some training.

That rep passes.

# MODULE 9 PASS STANDARD

A salesperson passes when they can:

- explain why closing begins during discovery
- identify the correct commercial next step
- distinguish strategy, implementation, and managed relationship
- present the current state accurately
- present ROI without guarantees
- explain theoretical vs recoverable value
- present a future-state workflow visually
- write a clear scope
- identify assumptions, dependencies, and exclusions
- identify third-party costs
- define client responsibilities
- define human review where necessary
- define success metrics and baselines
- present price calmly
- handle early price questions
- avoid unauthorized discounts
- close for the correct commitment
- conduct a field-sales next-step close
- navigate multi-stakeholder decisions
- know when to recommend a pilot
- create a clean implementation handoff
- know when the correct close is no

# MODULE 9 CORE RULE

The proposal should never be a surprise.

By the time the prospect sees the investment, they should understand:

THE PROBLEM
+
THE ECONOMICS
+
THE FUTURE STATE
+
THE SCOPE
+
THE RISKS
+
THE MEASUREMENT PLAN

Then the closing question is simple:

> Does solving this problem, in this way, justify this investment?

If yes:

Move forward responsibly.

If no:

Find out why.

If the economics, scope, risk, or feasibility do not hold up:

Do not force the sale.

A signed contract is not the goal.

A successful business outcome is.

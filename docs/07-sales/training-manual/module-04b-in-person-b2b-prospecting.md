# MODULE 4B - IN-PERSON B2B PROSPECTING

Status: Working Draft - Internal Sales Training

## Purpose

Your AI Department salespeople will not rely only on phones and email.

In many local and service industries, physically walking into a business can create trust faster than another cold call.

But in-person prospecting has a different social contract.

The rep is entering the prospect's workplace.

That means the rep must be:

- respectful
- brief
- observant
- professional
- non-disruptive
- honest about not having an appointment
- prepared to leave quickly if the timing is bad

The objective is not to corner an owner in the lobby.

The objective is to create enough relevance to earn a real conversation.

---

# 1. THE FIELD PROSPECTING DOCTRINE

Phone prospecting can survive a slightly rough opening.

In-person prospecting cannot.

When you walk into someone's business, your professionalism is part of the product.

The rep should communicate:

> I understand business. I respect your operation. I may have something relevant. I am not here to waste your time.

---

# 2. NEVER PRETEND YOU HAVE AN APPOINTMENT

Do not say:

> I am here to see John.

if John is not expecting you.

Do not imply:

> He knows what this is about.

if he does not.

Do not fake a delivery, referral, or existing relationship.

Default opening:

> Hey, I am [Rep] with Your AI Department. I do not have an appointment, and I do not want to interrupt anybody. We work with businesses on things like missed opportunities, follow-up, employee capacity, and system workflow. Who normally owns that side of the business here?

Honesty lowers resistance.

---

# 3. WHAT TO BRING

The field rep should look prepared without carrying a suitcase of brochures.

Recommended field kit:

- clean business cards
- one concise YAD leave-behind
- compact ROI field kit
- tablet or laptop if appropriate
- notepad / pen
- current approved scheduling / assessment QR code when finalized
- industry-specific field card when available

Do NOT hand the prospect the entire master training manual.

Do NOT dump fourteen ROI forms on the front desk.

Use the smallest relevant asset.

---

# 4. APPEARANCE AND BODY LANGUAGE

The rep should look appropriate for the industry.

A collision shop, roofing office, law firm, dental practice, and manufacturing plant may require different dress expectations.

General standard:

- clean
- professional
- approachable
- no overpowering cologne
- no chewing gum
- phone away
- shoulders open
- normal eye contact
- do not lean over the front desk
- do not invade employee workspace

Do not create the impression that you are conducting an inspection.

---

# 5. WHEN TO WALK IN

Use judgment.

Avoid obvious rush periods where possible.

Examples:

Restaurants:
Avoid lunch and dinner rush.

Dental / medical:
Avoid creating disruption at patient check-in.

Auto repair / collision:
Avoid immediately demanding the owner when the front office is handling a customer pileup.

Home services:
Mid-morning or mid-afternoon may be better than first dispatch rush.

Law firms / professional services:
Respect reception and scheduled-client environments.

If the room is visibly chaotic, shorten the visit.

---

# 6. THE FIRST 20 SECONDS

Recommended default:

> Hey, I am [Rep] with Your AI Department. I do not have an appointment, so I will keep this short. We help businesses identify where leads, follow-up, employee time, or reporting are falling through the cracks. I had one question about how you guys handle [relevant process]. Who would be the right person for that?

The opening should answer:

- who are you?
- why are you here?
- are you going to take forever?
- who needs to deal with you?

---

# 7. FRONT-DESK / RECEPTIONIST HANDLING

Treat the receptionist as a professional, not an obstacle.

## If they ask what you are selling

> Fair question. I am not here to sell a box of software at the front desk. We look at business workflows and figure out whether things like missed leads, follow-up, admin workload, reporting, or disconnected systems are costing the company enough to justify fixing. I am trying to figure out who owns that area here.

## If they say the owner is busy

> No problem. I expected that. Is there a better day or time to catch them for two minutes, or should I leave a short note?

## If they offer an email

> Perfect. What is the owner's or manager's name so I can make the note relevant instead of sending generic marketing?

Do not pressure the receptionist to disclose information they are uncomfortable sharing.

---

# 8. IF THE OWNER APPEARS

Do not suddenly launch a ten-minute monologue.

Use:

> Thanks for giving me a second. I will give you the 30-second version and you can tell me if it is irrelevant.

Then:

> We work with businesses on things like missed leads, slow follow-up, repetitive office work, CRM and system gaps, and marketing attribution. We are not trying to replace employees. We are looking for expensive workflow problems where better systems could make the team more capable. I was curious how you handle [one specific process] today.

Then stop talking.

---

# 9. THE ONE-QUESTION RULE

The first objective is not a presentation.

Ask one good question.

Examples:

Home services:

> When calls come in after hours or everyone is tied up, what happens today?

Collision / automotive:

> What happens to estimates that do not schedule immediately?

Law:

> What happens when a new intake comes in after hours or nobody reaches them on the first attempt?

Dental / med spa:

> What happens to a treatment inquiry that does not book on the first contact?

Property management:

> Which tenant or owner communications still create the most repetitive office work?

General B2B:

> If your volume increased by 50%, where would the office have to add people first?

One relevant question can earn five more minutes.

A five-minute pitch often earns the door.

---

# 10. OBSERVE WITHOUT ACTING LIKE A CONSULTANT BEFORE YOU ARE HIRED

In-person visits naturally reveal workflow clues.

You may notice:

- phones ringing repeatedly
- paper intake forms
- staff copying information
- whiteboards used as pipelines
- customers waiting for updates
- stacks of estimates / documents
- handwritten scheduling
- repeated receptionist questions
- obvious rush periods

These are conversation clues, not proof of dysfunction.

Do not say:

> I can already see your systems are a mess.

Instead:

> I noticed the phones stay pretty active here. Do you have a way to track what happens to calls nobody can grab immediately?

Observation should create a question, not an insult.

---

# 11. THE THREE-MINUTE FIELD CONVERSATION

If the owner gives you a few minutes, use:

## 1. WHAT HAPPENS?

> Walk me through what happens when [trigger] occurs.

## 2. WHERE DOES IT BREAK?

> Where does that process get inconsistent or depend heavily on someone remembering?

## 3. HOW OFTEN?

> Roughly how many times a week or month does that happen?

## 4. WHAT IS IT WORTH?

> What is one legitimate customer / job / appointment normally worth?

## 5. WHAT HAPPENS TODAY?

> What have you already tried?

## 6. NEXT STEP

> That sounds worth measuring properly instead of guessing in the lobby.

Do not attempt a complete operational audit standing at reception.

---

# 12. USING THE ROI FIELD KIT IN PERSON

Do not pull out a calculator before the owner agrees a problem exists.

Correct sequence:

Problem
->
Process
->
Frequency
->
Value
->
Simple math

Then:

> I have a one-page worksheet we use so we do not turn this into made-up ROI. Want to run the rough numbers together?

This makes the form collaborative rather than sales theater.

Use the relevant page only.

---

# 13. WHAT NOT TO SAY ABOUT EMPLOYEES

Never stand in front of an employee and tell the owner:

> AI can replace her.

Never say:

> You probably do not need this many people.

Never say:

> We could automate that person's job.

Use:

> The question is whether the current team could handle more volume if repetitive work were reduced.

> This may be a capacity problem rather than a staffing problem.

> We would keep the human judgment where it belongs and look at the routine work around it.

Employees may become internal champions if they hear that YAD is there to remove frustrating work rather than remove them.

---

# 14. INDUSTRY-SPECIFIC WALK-IN ANGLES

These are opening categories, not final vertical scripts.

After-hours calls
Dispatch / scheduling
Estimate follow-up
Service agreements
Review requests
Office workload
Marketing attribution

> When emergency calls hit after hours or the office is already slammed, how do you make sure the legitimate new jobs do not disappear?

Unsold estimates

## Collision / PDR / Automotive

Missed calls
Estimate scheduling

---

# 15. WHEN THE OWNER SAYS "SHOW ME"

Do not overpromise.

If you have an approved example relevant to the workflow, demonstrate it.

If you do not:

> I can show you the type of workflow we mean, but I do not want to pretend we know exactly what should be built for your company before understanding your systems.

The rep should never invent a live capability.

---

# 16. LEAVE-BEHIND STRATEGY

A leave-behind should help the prospect remember the conversation.

It should NOT try to explain the entire company.

Ideal leave-behind contains:

- YAD positioning
- five business leak categories
- a few diagnostic questions
- one simple workflow / ROI visual
- contact information
- current approved next-step CTA

Industry-specific leave-behinds may later include the top problems for that vertical.

---

# 17. FOLLOW-UP AFTER A WALK-IN

The follow-up should reference the visit.

Weak:

> Just following up to see if you had time to review our services.

Better:

> Good meeting you for a minute yesterday. You mentioned that estimate follow-up depends on each salesperson. I wanted to send the one-page example we discussed and see if it is worth mapping the actual numbers.

The rep should log:

- who they met
- role
- exact problem language
- next step
- best contact method
- timing
- any requested material

---

# 18. FIELD ROUTE PLANNING

In-person prospecting is expensive if a rep drives randomly.

Plan routes by:

- geography
- industry cluster
- prospect quality
- likely decision-maker presence
- time of day
- follow-up visits
- scheduled appointments nearby

A rep should be able to combine:

- planned cold stops
- follow-up stops
- scheduled meetings

into one efficient route.

Do not measure field performance only by number of doors entered.

Measure conversation quality and pipeline created.

---

# 19. FIELD PROSPECTING OUTCOMES

Track:

- businesses visited
- decision-maker identified
- decision-maker conversation
- meaningful pain identified
- follow-up permission
- discovery / strategy conversation scheduled
- disqualified
- return visit requested
- do not contact / do not return

Also track the problem category discovered.

This helps management learn which industries and messages are producing real opportunities.

---

# 20. SAFETY AND PROFESSIONAL BOUNDARIES

Do not enter restricted work areas without permission.

Do not walk into shop floors, treatment areas, kitchens, warehouses, or employee-only spaces uninvited.

Do not photograph business operations or customer information without permission.

Do not look at screens, documents, patient information, legal files, financial records, or customer data that are not being shown to you intentionally.

Respect posted no-soliciting rules and requests to leave.

The brand is more important than one appointment.

---

# 21. FIELD ROLEPLAY DRILLS

Every rep should practice:

- busy receptionist
- friendly receptionist
- owner appears unexpectedly
- owner gives 30 seconds
- owner gives three minutes
- owner says "leave something"
- owner says "email me"
- owner says "we already have a receptionist"
- owner says "we already use AI"
- employee becomes defensive
- visible operational chaos
- no obvious pain
- meaningful pain with no data
- clear opportunity and booking the next step

Roleplays should include body language, not only words.

---

# 22. FIELD SCORECARD

Managers should grade:

1. Appearance / professionalism
2. Respect for workplace
3. Honest introduction
4. Ability to state relevance quickly
5. Gatekeeper treatment
6. Quality of first question
7. Observation without insulting
8. Listening
9. Business / financial language
10. Employee-safe positioning
11. Appropriate use of ROI worksheet
12. No invented claims
13. Clear next step
14. Clean exit
15. CRM / field-note accuracy

---

# 23. MODULE 4B PASS STANDARD

A salesperson passes when they can walk into a business cold and:

- introduce themselves honestly
- avoid disrupting operations
- navigate reception professionally
- earn a short owner conversation
- ask one relevant operational question
- turn pain into a measurable business discussion when appropriate
- explain YAD without leading with AI jargon
- protect employee trust
- use one-page field tools without overwhelming the prospect
- leave behind useful material
- book a next step when justified
- leave professionally when the timing or fit is poor

The best field rep does not feel like a door-to-door software salesperson.

They feel like someone who understands how businesses lose money and capacity - and knows how to investigate whether better systems can fix it.

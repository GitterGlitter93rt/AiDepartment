Logistics companies should begin with high-friction workflows where people repeatedly gather information, communicate status, process documents, update systems, or coordinate exceptions — not with anything resembling autonomous transportation operations. The goal is reducing avoidable administrative friction, not replacing the operational and safety judgment that keeps freight moving safely.

## Candidate Starting Areas

Customer-service communication, shipment-status workflows, dispatch administration, document handling, sales follow-up, internal knowledge, recruiting, reporting, and billing or administrative support are all reasonable places to look for a first use case.

## Prioritization

Weigh transaction volume, how much manual effort a workflow currently requires, whether the process is consistent enough to automate reliably, whether the underlying data is actually available, integration complexity, the safety consequence of getting it wrong, the customer consequence, and how much human review the use case genuinely needs.

## Start Around an Existing System

A TMS, a CRM, an ERP, fleet systems, customer portals, document repositories, calendars, and communication platforms are all reasonable anchor points — building around a system you already use is usually faster and safer than starting from scratch.

## Controlled Rollout

One workflow, with defined users, a measured outcome, a clear human escalation path, and room to improve before expanding further is a far better pattern than trying to transform multiple processes simultaneously.

## Human Judgment

Driving, safety decisions, hazardous-material handling, regulated operations, equipment condition assessments, and other high-consequence operational decisions remain human responsibilities, regardless of how mature the surrounding automation becomes.

The best AI opportunities are not necessarily the most technically impressive. They are business problems where improvement matters economically and where the process is actually suitable for AI, automation, or better systems. This guide walks through a practical way to build a shortlist of opportunities based on business impact, feasibility, risk, and measurability.

## Begin With Friction

Start by looking for friction: delays, repetitive work, manual handoffs, missed follow-up, information-retrieval problems, inconsistent processes, high administrative burden, unstructured information, and bottlenecks that limit growth. These are the places where a business is already spending time and energy fighting the same problem repeatedly.

## Separate Interesting AI Ideas From Valuable Business Opportunities

An interesting AI demo is not necessarily a worthwhile implementation. Before getting attached to an idea, evaluate it against how frequently the underlying task occurs, its economic impact, how labor-intensive it is, its effect on customers, how well it would scale, the risk involved, and the effort required to implement it. Ideas that sound impressive in a demo often fail one or more of these tests.

## Score Opportunities Across Four Dimensions

A simple scoring framework can help separate genuine opportunities from distractions:

**Business Impact** — How meaningful would improvement actually be?

**Feasibility** — Can the workflow, data, integrations, and tools realistically support implementation?

**Risk** — What happens when the system gets it wrong?

**Measurability** — Can improvement actually be measured, or would results be a matter of opinion?

## Look Across the Entire Business

Opportunities can exist in sales, marketing, customer service, operations, finance, HR, training, knowledge management, leadership, and administrative workflows. That doesn't mean every department needs AI — it means the search for opportunity shouldn't be limited to whichever department happens to be top of mind.

## Prioritize Repeated Work

A small improvement to a process performed thousands of times can matter more than a dramatic improvement to a process performed twice a year. When comparing opportunities, frequency deserves as much weight as how dramatic the improvement looks on paper.

## Evaluate Data and System Readiness

Before committing to an opportunity, it's worth asking some practical questions: Where does the information actually live? Is the data structured? Is it reliable? Are the relevant systems integrated? Is the information sensitive? Who owns the workflow? Can a human realistically review the output? The answers often determine whether an otherwise-promising opportunity is actually ready for AI.

## Account for Change Management

A technically successful system can still fail if employees don't understand, trust, or use it. Workflow ownership, training, leadership support, governance, usability, and measurement all affect whether a technically sound implementation actually changes how work gets done.

Automation should not be treated as automatically beneficial. Some workflows require human judgment, accountability, empathy, context, legal review, or careful escalation. The goal is not maximum automation — the goal is the right division of work between people and technology.

## Automation Is a Business Decision, Not a Technology Goal

Maximum automation can create real risk: poor customer experiences, operational fragility, and decisions made without the context a person would have brought to them. Automation should be evaluated the same way any other business decision is — by weighing its benefits against its risks — rather than pursued simply because it's technically possible.

## High-Stakes Decisions Need Human Accountability

Legal decisions, financial approvals, hiring or termination decisions, safety-critical decisions, major contractual commitments, and sensitive customer disputes typically need a person who is accountable for the outcome. This is not legal advice — it's a general principle that high-consequence decisions usually benefit from a human decision-maker, whatever your specific legal and regulatory obligations require.

## Ambiguous Situations Often Need Human Judgment

AI performs best where the task and context are sufficiently well-defined. When a situation is unusual or uncertain, the safer default is to escalate it to a person rather than let a system guess.

## Sensitive Customer Conversations May Need People

Complaints, escalations, emotionally difficult conversations, situations with large financial consequences, and complex negotiations are often better handled by a person. AI can still help — by assisting with preparation or routing the conversation to the right person — without replacing the human interaction itself.

## Do Not Automate a Process You Do Not Understand

Before automating anything, it helps to document the process as it actually works: its inputs, the decisions being made, the exceptions that come up, who owns it, what output is expected, and how it fails when something goes wrong. Automating a process nobody fully understands tends to produce a faster version of the same problems.

## Poor Data Can Make Automation Dangerous

Automation built on top of inaccurate, incomplete, outdated, or inconsistent data doesn't fix the underlying data problem — it just executes on bad information faster and with less scrutiny than a person would have applied.

## Some Tasks Should Be AI-Assisted, Not AI-Controlled

A useful pattern is to have AI handle the first pass and a person handle the judgment: AI drafts, a human reviews and approves; AI summarizes, a human interprets; AI recommends, a human decides; AI flags, a human investigates. This keeps the efficiency benefit of AI without removing human accountability from the outcome.

## Use Escalation Thresholds

Systems should have clearly defined cases where they stop and request human intervention — uncertainty, a policy exception, a large financial impact, sensitive information, an unusual customer request, or a low-confidence output are all reasonable triggers for escalation rather than letting the system proceed on its own.

## A Better Question Than "Can We Automate This?"

A more useful question than "can we automate this?" is: "Which parts of this process should technology handle, and which parts require human judgment?"

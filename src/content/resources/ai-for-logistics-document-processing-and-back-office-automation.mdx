Transportation and logistics runs on paper that is no longer paper. Bills of lading, proofs of delivery, carrier invoices, rate confirmations, customs paperwork, customer instructions, lumper receipts, accessorial claims — every one of them arrives in a different format, from a different party, through a different channel, and has to be read, classified, entered into a system, checked against something else, and filed somewhere a person can find it in nine months.

The work is high volume, low judgment, and unglamorous, which makes it a genuinely good candidate for automation. It is also full of documents where a wrong number costs real money, which makes "let the AI handle it" a bad plan. This is about where the line sits.

## The Documents, and Why They Are Not Interchangeable

The instinct is to treat this as one problem — "process our documents". It is not. Each type fails differently.

**Bills of lading.** The BOL is the shipment's identity document: shipper, consignee, addresses, piece count, weight, freight class, PO numbers, special instructions. It arrives as a clean PDF from a portal, as a photograph taken in a cab, as a fax that has been through three machines, or as a scan of a carbon copy where the third page is the one you can actually read. Handwritten piece counts and hand-added notes in the margin are normal, not exceptional.

**Proofs of delivery.** A POD's job is to answer two questions: was it delivered, and was anything wrong. The signature and the date are the easy part. The value is in the exception written across it — *short 2 cartons*, *refused*, *damaged, driver noted*, *delivered to dock, no signature obtained*. Those notes are the reason PODs get audited, they are almost always handwritten, and they are exactly the part a naive extraction pipeline skips.

**Freight invoices.** A carrier invoice is a set of claims about money: linehaul, fuel surcharge, accessorials, detention, and a reference to a shipment. Every line is a claim that has to agree with something you already believe — a rate confirmation, a contract, a tariff. This is where extraction alone produces almost nothing of value; the value is entirely in the comparison.

**Rate confirmations and customer instructions.** Free-form, frequently email bodies rather than attachments, and full of conditions that matter — appointment windows, driver requirements, temperature ranges, what to do if the receiver refuses.

A pipeline that treats all four as "documents" will do a mediocre job of all four. Classification first, then a specific extraction and validation path per type, is what makes it work.

## What AI Is Genuinely Good At Here

**Classification.** Deciding what a document is, before deciding what to do with it. This is the single highest-leverage step and the one most often skipped, because it looks trivial and is not: a POD and a BOL frequently share a layout, and a signed BOL often *is* the POD.

**Extraction into structured fields.** Pulling pro numbers, BOL numbers, PO numbers, dates, weights, piece counts, addresses and charge lines out of an unpredictable layout and into consistent fields. This is the part that has changed most in the last few years — layout-independent extraction genuinely works now in a way template-based tools never did.

**Reading the messy human parts.** Handwritten exception notes, stamps, margin annotations. Imperfectly, and with much lower confidence than printed text, but a system that surfaces *"there is handwriting here, and it may say 'short 2'"* is far more useful than one that silently ignores it.

**Matching and reconciliation.** Comparing an invoice line against a rate confirmation, a POD against a shipment record, a piece count against what was tendered. Flagging what disagrees.

**Summarising and routing.** Turning a four-page email chain of customer instructions into the five facts dispatch needs, and getting it to the right desk.

**Naming and filing.** Consistent document naming and attachment to the correct shipment record — dull, and responsible for a surprising share of the time people lose.

## OCR and Language Models Do Different Jobs

Treating these as competing options is a common and expensive mistake. They belong in the same pipeline doing different work.

**OCR turns pixels into characters.** It has no opinion about meaning. Modern OCR is very good on clean printed text and materially worse on skewed photographs, low-contrast fax output, dot-matrix print, and handwriting. Critically, good OCR gives you a **per-character or per-region confidence score**, and that number is the most useful signal in the entire pipeline.

**A language model turns characters into meaning.** It decides that this string is the consignee and that one is the bill-to, that "P/U 8/14 0600-1000" is an appointment window, that this charge line is detention. It handles layout variation that would break a template.

The failure mode differs, and this is the part worth internalising: **OCR fails visibly and a language model fails plausibly.** Bad OCR produces `l0O5` and looks wrong. A language model handed `l0O5` will confidently return a well-formatted, entirely wrong number, and it will look exactly like a correct answer.

That single asymmetry is the argument for everything in the next two sections. Nobody should describe an extraction pipeline as accurate without saying what happens when it is not.

## Confidence Thresholds Are a Business Decision

The useful question is not "how accurate is it". It is **"what does it do when it is unsure, and who decided that."**

Every extracted field should carry a confidence value, and every field should have a threshold attached to it — set by the business, not by whoever configured the tool:

- **High confidence, low stakes** — a reference number that will be validated against the TMS anyway. Write it, log it, move on.
- **High confidence, high stakes** — an invoice amount, a piece count on a claim. Write it, but reconcile it against another source before anyone acts on it.
- **Low confidence, any stakes** — exception queue. Always.
- **Anything handwritten on a POD** — treat as low confidence regardless of what the model reports, because a model's confidence in its reading of handwriting is not well calibrated.

Thresholds should differ by field and by document type. One global setting means either drowning the exception queue or letting invoice errors through, and both of those failures get blamed on "the AI" rather than on the configuration decision that actually caused them.

## Validation Is Where the Value Is

Extraction without validation moves errors faster. It does not reduce them.

Four layers, in increasing order of usefulness:

1. **Format.** Is this a plausible date, a plausible weight, a pro number in the carrier's format? Catches the worst OCR failures for almost nothing.
2. **Internal consistency.** Do the charge lines sum to the invoice total? Does the piece count agree between page one and the continuation sheet?
3. **Cross-source.** Does the invoice agree with the rate confirmation? Does the POD's shipment reference match a real shipment, with a delivery date that is not before the pickup date? This layer catches the plausible-looking wrong answers that format checks never will.
4. **Human review.** For everything the first three cannot settle, and for everything above the stakes threshold regardless of confidence.

The third layer is the one that earns the project. It is also the one that requires the integration work described below, which is why "we'll add the TMS connection in phase two" tends to quietly remove most of the benefit from phase one.

## Exception Handling Is the Product

A document pipeline is judged by its exception queue, not by its happy path. Getting 88% of documents through cleanly is a good outcome; whether the project succeeds depends entirely on what the other 12% do.

An exception queue that works:

- Shows the person the **document and the extracted values side by side**, with the uncertain field highlighted. Not a spreadsheet of rejects.
- Says **why** it is an exception — low confidence, failed a cross-check, unknown document type, duplicate.
- Lets a correction be made **in one place**, and writes it through to the destination system.
- **Learns from corrections**, or at least reports them, so a field that is corrected constantly becomes visible as a pattern rather than a permanent tax.
- Has an **owner and a service level**. An unowned queue becomes a backlog, and a backlog becomes a workaround.

Plan the exception path first. It is the part people live with.

## Getting It Into the TMS or ERP

Extracted data that stays in the extraction tool has changed nothing. The integration is not the last step — it is what makes the earlier steps worth doing, because cross-source validation is impossible without it.

**Ingestion.** Most of this arrives by email attachment, so a monitored mailbox is usually the front door, alongside EDI, carrier portals, and driver app uploads. Deduplication belongs here: the same POD arriving twice, as an attachment and again in a portal sync, is routine.

**Writing back.** Direct API where the TMS supports one, a webhook or file drop where it does not. What matters more than the mechanism: idempotency, so a retried write does not create a second shipment document, and a clear rule for what happens when the target record does not exist yet.

**Matching.** Attaching a document to the right shipment is its own problem. Pro number, BOL number, PO number, and reference numbers all fail sometimes — reused, transposed, or absent. A matching strategy with a defined fallback order, and a defined behaviour when nothing matches, is worth designing deliberately rather than discovering.

## The Audit Trail

For anything touching money or liability — invoices, claims, PODs backing a shortage — you need to be able to answer, months later: what did the original document say, what did the system extract, how confident was it, what did it check against, did a person change it, who, and when.

That record is what makes the difference between an automated process a controller will sign off on and one that gets quietly switched off after the first dispute. It also makes the system improvable: without stored corrections, nobody can tell you which field is costing the most time.

## What Should Not Be Fully Automated

Some of these are judgment calls; the first three are not.

- **Disputing or approving a carrier invoice above a material threshold.** Flag the discrepancy, quantify it, put it in front of a person.
- **Deciding a claim.** Extraction supports the decision. It does not make it.
- **Customs and regulated filings.** The accuracy standard and the consequence of error are both set by someone other than you.
- **Anything where the document is the only record.** If there is nothing to cross-check against, a wrong extraction is undetectable until it causes a problem.
- **Reading a handwritten exception note as though it were structured data.** Surface it, attach the image, let a person decide.

A useful test before automating any step: *if this is wrong and nobody notices for thirty days, what happens?* Where the answer involves money leaving the business or a customer relationship ending, a person stays in the loop.

## Where to Start

Not with the hardest document. Start where volume is high, the format is relatively consistent, and there is something to validate against — usually POD filing and matching, or invoice line extraction where rate confirmations already exist in a system.

Measure three things from day one: how many documents clear without a person, how often the extraction was actually right on the ones that cleared (sample them — do not assume), and how long the exception queue takes to work. The second number is the one that is easiest to skip and most expensive to have been wrong about.

Done well, this removes a genuine daily grind from a back office. Done as a demo, it produces a confident stream of well-formatted numbers that nobody checked. The difference is validation, thresholds, and an exception queue somebody owns.
